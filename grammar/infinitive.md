# Infinitive

The infinitive names an action, like "to see" or "sitting". It usually comes after the prefix ל for "to", but it can also take ב, כ or מ, or stand alone. A verb with all three root letters forms it from its root letters alone, with no ending:

| Example | Root | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| לְהַדְלִיק | דלק | to light |
| לְקַדֵּשׁ | קדשׁ | to sanctify |
| לְהַלֵּל | הלל | to praise |

The vowels change from one verb pattern to another, but none of them adds an ending. Verbs that lose a root letter are different: they add an ending to the infinitive, shown in the next two sections.

## Roots ending in ה: ־וֹת

When the last root letter is ה, the ה drops out and the infinitive ends in ־וֹת instead:

| Ending | Example | Root | Translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| ־וֹת | לִרְא<b style="color:red">וֹת</b> | ראה | to see |
| ־וֹת | לְהוֹד<b style="color:red">וֹת</b> | ידה | to thank |
| ־וֹת | לַעֲשׂ<b style="color:red">וֹת</b> | עשה | to do |
| ־וֹת | לְהַרְא<b style="color:red">וֹת</b> | ראה | to show |

The final ה of these roots isn't a real consonant. It's a spelling for a vowel at the end of the word, standing in for an older י or ו. The ending ־וֹת appears in every verb pattern, so roots like this can be spotted by it: a verb form ending in ־וֹת with only two root letters before it almost always has ה as its missing third letter.

In the word for "to thank", the first holam comes from the root, not the ending. The root's first letter was originally ו. After the ה of this verb pattern it turns into the holam of הוֹ־, and the י only shows up in other forms of the root.

## Roots that lose their first letter: ־ת

When the first root letter is י or נ (and in the root הלך), that letter usually drops out, and the infinitive ends in ת instead:

| Ending | Example | Root | Note | Translation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ־ת | שֶׁבֶ<b style="color:red">ת</b> | ישׁב | the first י drops | sitting |
| ־ת | צֵא<b style="color:red">ת</b> | יצא | the first י drops | going out |
| ־ת | לָדַעַ<b style="color:red">ת</b> | ידע | the first י drops | to know |
| ־ת | לֶכֶ<b style="color:red">ת</b> | הלך | the first ה drops, as if it were י | walking |
| ־ת | גֶּשֶׁ<b style="color:red">ת</b> | נגשׁ | the first נ drops | approaching |
| ־ת | לָתֵ<b style="color:red">ת</b> | נתן | the first נ drops; the last נ merges into the ת | to give |

Many of these infinitives get two segols, as in the word for "sitting", or a patach before ע and ח, as in the word for "to know". In the word for "to give", the last נ merges into the ת of the ending, as described in [letter assimilation](assimilation.md). Only one ת is written, and the result keeps neither נ.

The ת stays when an ending is added, as in בְּשִׁבְתְּךָ (when you sit). See [object endings](object-endings.md#after-an-infinitive).

## Where the ending comes from

The ת of these infinitives is the same suffix that makes nouns feminine. It's also the suffix for abstract nouns, which often end in ה, a later form of the same ת:

| Ending | Example | Root | Translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| ־ה | אַהֲבָ<b style="color:red">ה</b> | אהב | love |
| ־ה | יִרְאָ<b style="color:red">ה</b> | ירא | fear |
| ־ת | דַּעַ<b style="color:red">ת</b> | ידע | knowledge |

The name "feminine" is misleading here. In the languages related to Hebrew (Aramaic, Arabic, Akkadian), this suffix is older than its use for gender. Originally it was a general suffix for making nouns, especially abstract nouns and nouns of action. Marking feminine nouns is only its best-known job. An infinitive is the name of an action, a verbal noun, so it takes the same suffix as other abstract nouns.

The ה at the end of these nouns was once a ת, and the ת still comes back in [construct forms](construct.md), as in אַהֲבַת (love of). Some of these nouns also work as infinitives in the Bible, such as לְאַהֲבָה (to love) and לְיִרְאָה (to fear). The word for "knowledge" is spelled the same as the infinitive "to know", without the ל.

The suffix is kept in these weak verbs because they would otherwise be too short. Once a root letter is lost, only two consonants are left, which is less than the usual shape of a noun. The ת fills the word out to a full noun shape. Verbs with all three root letters don't need the filler, so their infinitives have no ending.

The holam in ־וֹת is explained by a regular sound change. In the older form of the ending, the vowel before the ת was a long "a". In Hebrew a long "a" often became "o", which turned the ending into ־וֹת. This change is called the Canaanite shift. The same shift explains why some Hebrew words have a holam where Arabic has a long "a". The exact prehistory of the ending is still debated, but the ת as the noun suffix is the standard explanation.

## Watch out

Other forms end in the same letters, and a root ending in ה doesn't always mean an infinitive:

| Example | Root | Note | Translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| לִרְאוֹת | ראה | the infinitive: ל before, the root's ה replaced by the ending | to see |
| מִצְוֹת | צוה | the plural ending for feminine nouns, on a noun whose root also ends in ה | commandments |
| שֶׁבֶת | ישׁב | the infinitive: the root's first י dropped, the ת is the ending | sitting |
| שַׁבָּת | שׁבת | the ת is a root letter | Shabbat |

For the plural ending see [plural forms](plural.md). Before reading a final ת as the ending, check that a root letter is missing. If all three root letters are there, the ת belongs to the root or to a noun ending, as in [nouns built from roots](nouns.md).
